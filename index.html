Welcome

Welcome to SCOOT, ISCA's guide to online training resources in Speech Communication.

Our aim is to help and encourage students who are learning about Speech Communication, who may come from a variety of backgrounds: phonetics, linguistics, computer science, electrical engineering — and be at different stages in their education, from first degree to Ph.D.

SCOOT provides students in speech communication with a guide to high-quality, up-to-date learning resources available on the internet, which can be tailored to their individual needs.

Please note that in many cases we are picking up material which has been prepared for particular cohorts of students taking particular modules, and therefore you will find material which is not relevant for you, e.g. details of assignments, lecture timings and so on.

SCOOT will never be finished.

Phonetics

Original SCOOT page: isca-speech.org/Phonetics

Phonetics is the study of the sounds of speech. It is concerned with the physical properties of speech sounds or signs (phones): their physiological production, acoustic properties, auditory perception, and neurophysiological status. Phonology, on the other hand, is concerned with the abstract, grammatical characterization of systems of sounds or signs.

In the case of oral languages there are three basic areas of study — articulatory, acoustic, and auditory phonetics — inter-connected through the common mechanism of sound, such as wavelength (pitch), amplitude, and harmonics. [adapted from Wikipedia]

Speech Synthesis

Original SCOOT page: isca-speech.org/Speech-Synthesis

Most attention has been paid to 'Text-to-Speech' applications, i.e. type in the words you want and have them spoken for you. Speech synthesis systems are evaluated in terms of intelligibility (how many words are correctly identified by listeners?) and naturalness (to what extent does the synthesis resemble a normal human voice?).

Synthesis from written text (orthography) involves two stages, which are to a large extent independent: Text Analysis — transform the text into an intermediate representation — and Waveform Generation — render the speech acoustics from the intermediate representation.

Waveform Generation — Classic Speech Synthesis
  • Classic speech synthesis was based on simulations of the vocal tract, with filters for the formants. The simulation was supplied with parameters for excitation, formant frequencies and amplitudes. The best-known systems were the Klatt Synthesiser (US) and the Holmes Synthesiser (UK); tutorial papers for both are in the Smithsonian Speech Synthesis History Project (SSSHP), 1986–2002. The Klatt synthesiser is familiar as the voice of Stephen Hawking, and provided the original 'DECtalk' and Apple voices. [Smithsonian Speech Synthesis History Project Klatt Holmes]
Waveform Generation — Concatenative Synthesis
  • Speech is produced by joining recorded acoustic segments together. There are ways of doing this which fool the auditory system into believing it is hearing flowing speech from the same speaker — see the synthesis lecture in Stanford's CS224S course. [Stanford CS224S syllabus (archived copy)]
Waveform Generation — Parametric (HMM-based) Synthesis
  • Synthesis based on statistical models (somewhat) similar to those developed for speech recognition. Simon King: 'A beginners' guide to statistical parametric speech synthesis'. [A beginners' guide to statistical parametric speech synthesis]
  • Simon King's inaugural lecture: 'Using Speech Synthesis to give Everyone their own Voice'. [Inaugural lecture]

  • The first ASR technique to get anywhere: DTW is based on finding the best time-alignment between a stored template (e.g. the features for a known word) and a pattern (e.g. the features for an incoming word). About

SCOOT is ISCA's guide to online training resources in Speech Communication, created and long maintained by members of the ISCA community (the archived pages credit course material by Roger Moore, Simon King and many others). Its motto: 'SCOOT will never be finished.'

This site — SCOOT 2.0 — is a prototype rebuild of that guide. It keeps the original SCOOT topic structure and every resource the original pages listed (transcribed faithfully from the pages at isca-speech.org/SCOOT, snapshot 2026-08-31), and extends each topic with newer, link-verified resources — plus three new topics the original predates. Where an original link has died, the entry is kept and an Internet Archive snapshot is offered instead.

It serves the same audience as the original: students of speech communication arriving from phonetics, linguistics, computer science, or electrical engineering, anywhere between a first degree and a PhD.

Since 31 August 2026 the page also hosts the ISCA SIGs & online events part — an atlas of what each of ISCA's 20 Special Interest Groups is doing, with verified links to their recorded webinars and lectures. It previously lived at a separate address, which now redirects here.

This prototype is for evaluation by the ISCA education community. It is not (yet) an official ISCA publication, and no endorsement is implied by inclusion of any resource.

How this site is maintained

Original entries (marked ⬤ Original SCOOT) are faithful transcriptions of the original pages — same resources, same descriptions lightly edited for typos. The HTML snapshots they were transcribed from are preserved with the project source materials and audited mechanically against this page.

Extension entries (marked ✚ 2.0) were curated by an AI curator: found, link-checked and fact-checked before listing. Every catalog link on this page — original and extension — is covered by the link-status report; its latest verification time appears at the bottom of this page. Dead originals carry an 'archived copy' link to the Internet Archive. Links in the SIG part were each reviewed on 31 August 2026 as described under 'The SIG part' below.

Every editorial change is recorded in the changelog below, including changes made in response to community suggestions.

The SIG part

The SIG part was compiled 31 August 2026 from the official ISCA SIG list, each SIG's own website, and targeted searches. Every video link was reviewed. Most were fetched directly; links blocked by consent walls, bot protection, or broken TLS were checked through first-party index pages, direct video metadata, or YouTube oEmbed and are described with their verification limits. A second, independent pass re-checked every link and actively searched for recordings the first pass might have missed; one dead video link was removed.

The ISCA SIGs & online events part of this page is an independent reference, not an official ISCA publication. It was researched and compiled by an AI assistant (Claude) at the request of an ISCA board member. Facts reflect what the linked sources stated on 31 August 2026; corrections welcome.
