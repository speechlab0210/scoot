About the original SCOOT catalog

Welcome to SCOOT, ISCA's guide to online training resources in Speech Communication.

Our aim is to help and encourage students who are learning about Speech Communication, who may come from a variety of backgrounds: phonetics, linguistics, computer science, electrical engineering… and be at different stages in their education, from first degree to Ph.D.

SCOOT provides students in speech communication with a guide to high-quality, up-to-date learning resources available on the internet, which can be tailored to their individual needs.

Please note that in many cases we are picking up material which has been prepared for particular cohorts of students taking particular modules, and therefore you will find material which is not relevant for you, e.g. details of assignments, lecture timings and so on.

SCOOT will never be finished.

Linguistics

Original SCOOT page: isca-speech.org/Linguistics

Linguistics is the scientific study of language and involves an analysis of language form, language meaning, and language in context. Psycholinguistics is the study of the relationships between linguistic behaviour and psychological processes, including the process of language acquisition. Phonetics is the study of speech sounds. Phonology is the organisation of speech sounds in a language.

See also: Linguistics (Wikipedia)

Phonetics

Original SCOOT page: isca-speech.org/Phonetics

Phonetics is the study of the sounds of speech. It is concerned with the physical properties of speech sounds or signs (phones): their physiological production, acoustic properties, auditory perception, and neurophysiological status. Phonology, on the other hand, is concerned with the abstract, grammatical characterization of systems of sounds or signs.

In the case of oral languages there are three basic areas of study — articulatory, acoustic, and auditory phonetics — inter-connected through the common mechanism of sound, such as wavelength (pitch), amplitude, and harmonics. [adapted from Wikipedia]

● Original SCOOT · 3

Speech Synthesis

Original SCOOT page: isca-speech.org/Speech-Synthesis

Most attention has been paid to 'Text-to-Speech' applications, i.e. type in the words you want and have them spoken for you. Speech synthesis systems are evaluated in terms of intelligibility (how many words are correctly identified by listeners?) and naturalness (to what extent does the synthesis resemble a normal human voice?).

Synthesis from written text (orthography) involves 2 stages: Text Analysis – transform the text into an intermediate representation; Waveform Generation – render the speech acoustics from the intermediate representation. To a large extent these stages are independent.

● Original SCOOT · 15

Waveform Generation — Classic Speech Synthesis
  • Classic speech synthesis was based on simulations of the vocal tract, with filters for the formants. The simulation was supplied with parameters for excitation, formant frequencies and amplitudes. There were tables specifying how the parameters should change with time for different phones. The technique was also called 'Speech Synthesis by Rule' or 'Formant Synthesis'. The best known systems were the Klatt Synthesiser (US) and the Holmes Synthesiser (UK). Tutorial papers for both systems are in the Smithsonian Speech Synthesis History Project (SSSHP) 1986–2002. See this link for Klatt and this link for Holmes. The Klatt synthesiser is familiar as the voice of Stephen Hawking, and provided the original 'DECtalk' and 'Apple Voices'. [Smithsonian Speech Synthesis History Project Klatt Mattingly 1974 (the original page's 'Holmes' link)]
Waveform Generation — Concatenative Synthesis
  • Speech is produced by joining recorded acoustic segments together. There are ways of doing this which fool the auditory system into believing it is hearing flowing speech from the same speaker, notably PSOLA (Pitch Synchronous Overlap and Add). See the May 22 lecture by Andrew Maas, starting at slide 18. [Stanford CS224S syllabus (archived copy)]

DNNs for ASR
  • DNN feature extraction: Wav2vec and Wav2vec2 are schemes for 'Learning the Structure of Speech from Raw Audio'. A convolutional network is trained by auto-encoding (i.e. to predict the next item given the history). This unsupervised training is then followed by supervised training on a much smaller dataset. The resulting feature extractor outperforms conventional feature sets as a module within an ASR. Wav2vec2 was developed by Meta. A 'Lazy Science' guide to Wav2vec2. [A 'Lazy Data Science' guide to wav2vec 2.0]
  • Meta's guide to Wav2Vec2. [Meta's guide to wav2vec 2.0]
  • Sequence modelling: ASR is a sequence-to-sequence task. Such tasks can be handled by recurrent neural networks, where at each step the network has access to the next frame of input data and to feedback from the last step. Recurrent neural nets have some serious problems and in recent years they have been superseded by Transformer architectures using attention-based models, introduced by Google in a famous paper 'Attention Is All You Need'. Transformer models designed for speech recognition are usually based on the encoder-decoder architecture similar to seq2seq models. In more detail, they are based on the self-attention mechanism instead of the recurrence that is adopted by RNNs. In end-to-end ASR the transformation from audio to words is accomplished by DNNs alone, with no use of phonetic or linguistic knowledge. An animated explanation of the 'Attention Is All You Need' paper by Jean de Dieu Nyandwi of CMU. [Animated explanation of 'Attention Is All You Need']

About

SCOOT is ISCA's guide to online training resources in Speech Communication, created and long maintained by members of the ISCA community (the archived pages credit course material by Roger Moore, Simon King and many others). Its motto: 'SCOOT will never be finished.'

SCOOT 2.0 preserves the original topic structure and all 105 resource entries from the original ISCA SCOOT pages as captured on 31 August 2026, alongside clearly marked extensions. Original entries keep their descriptions. Where an original link broke or its domain was taken over by an unrelated site, it now points to the same resource's current address or to an archived copy, and each repair is listed in the changelog.

It serves the same audience as the original: students of speech communication arriving from phonetics, linguistics, computer science, or electrical engineering, anywhere between a first degree and a PhD.

The ISCA SIG directory, seminars, events and recordings moved to the independent ISCA SIG Atlas on 3 October 2026. Use the direct link at the top of this page. The Spoken LLM Benchmark Atlas also has its own dedicated page.

This is NOT an official ISCA publication (see the notice at the top of the page for how the project began). This rebuilt catalog has not been verified by a human, and listing a resource does not imply endorsement.

How this site is maintained

Original entries (marked ● Original SCOOT) are transcriptions of the original ISCA SCOOT pages: the same resources and descriptions, with typos corrected and a few documented differences (listed in data/original.json in the GitHub repository). The archived pages they were transcribed from are kept with the project's working files, and before every update a script checks that each resource link on those pages is still present here or recorded as a repair.

Every day, early morning Taipei time, an AI agent checks every link in the catalog and compares each linked page with the version it last reviewed. GitHub projects, Hugging Face datasets and YouTube videos are checked through their public interfaces. A link is labelled as possibly unavailable after it has failed on three daily checks in a row, or when a manual check found it unavailable, and an archived copy is linked where one exists. A link whose domain now redirects to unrelated sites, such as gambling pages, is replaced with an archived copy of the original page, and the build refuses to link that domain again.

Each day a few topics are also reviewed in depth, rotating through all of them: the pages behind their extension entries are re-read, version and status details are updated, and notable new learning resources may be added. A change is made only when the resource's own page supports it. Larger changes, such as new topics, a different structure or removing original entries, are referred to the project's human supervisor instead of being made automatically.

Extension entries are marked “SCOOT 2.0 extensions”. The footer shows when links were last checked. Check linked course pages for current availability, pricing and versions.

Every content change is listed in the changelog below, including changes made in response to suggestions.
